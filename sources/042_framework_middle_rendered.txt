## Middle School

**Epsilon Middle School — quantitative thesis / longitudinal observational design**

Applying a Theoretical and Conceptual Framework to a Quantitative Thesis

**Illustrative thesis title:** *Adult Support, Identity Acceptance, and Subsequent Attendance at Epsilon Middle: A Quantitative Study of Leadership-Relevant School Conditions*

> **Status of this example**
>
> Epsilon is a fictional teaching district. School figures are synthetic; proposed study materials, participant excerpts, statistical results, findings, and recommendations are illustrative unless explicitly identified otherwise. They are not evidence collected from actual participants or schools. Published scholarship and institutional guidance are cited separately; neither is an Epsilon finding.

The application does not contain the student-level survey and linked attendance records needed to conduct this proposed study.

The qualitative Epsilon Elementary example asked **how families and school personnel negotiate attendance support**. This quantitative example asks a related but different question:

> **Are students’ reported experiences of adult support and identity acceptance associated with their subsequent attendance, and how are those experiences distributed across the school?**

The leadership model remains intact. The researcher describes the pattern, examines variation, identifies the evidence boundary, incorporates community knowledge, investigates institutional conditions, and determines what action or further inquiry is justified. Quantitative methods change how evidence is collected and analyzed; they do not remove those responsibilities.

### 1. Establish the problem without assuming its explanation

Epsilon Middle’s synthetic data show elevated chronic absenteeism alongside academic concerns. The latest chronic-absenteeism rates are calculated from the whole-student counts generated by the application.

**Table 1. Epsilon Middle’s synthetic context, 2024–25**

| **Reported population** | **Chronically absent / eligible students** | **Chronic absenteeism** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Schoolwide | 156 / 720 | 21.7% |
| English Learners | 85 / 250 | 34.0% |
| Students with Disabilities | 41 / 110 | 37.3% |
| Foster Youth | 13 / 28 | 46.4% |
| Socioeconomically Disadvantaged | 126 / 430 | 29.3% |

*Note.* These are fabricated teaching data, not observations about a real school. Groups overlap, and the schoolwide population includes the subgroup students. The rows therefore are not independent, mutually exclusive samples. The rates reflect the application’s count-generation and rounding rules rather than its initial target percentages. Source: the app’s epsilon_synthetic.py teaching-data definitions.

The application also gives Epsilon Middle a synthetic schoolwide mathematics result of **30.9% meeting or exceeding standard (124/401 valid scores)**, compared with **11.8% for English Learners (15/127), 10.3% for Students with Disabilities (6/58), and 5.0% for Foster Youth (1/20)**. These percentages are calculated from generated whole-student achievement counts and valid-score denominators, not from the attendance denominators in Table 1. They remain fabricated teaching data, not actual student results.

These patterns justify attention, but they do not establish that weak adult relationships, exclusion, instructional access, family circumstances, or any other particular condition caused the outcomes.

For a manageable thesis, this example makes **attendance the primary outcome**. Mathematics remains part of the problem’s context rather than becoming a second major analysis. That narrowing allows the researcher to develop a coherent framework and collect evidence specifically suited to the research questions.

#### Model problem statement

The problem addressed by this study is the limited understanding of the relationship between students’ experiences of adult support, their perceptions of identity acceptance, and subsequent attendance at Epsilon Middle. Although the school’s synthetic indicators identify attendance disparities, they do not measure these school experiences or establish their relationship with individual students’ attendance.

The proposed study therefore moves beyond documenting subgroup differences to examining leadership-relevant conditions that students may experience differently within the same school.

#### Model purpose statement

The purpose of this nonexperimental, longitudinal quantitative study is to estimate the association between students’ early-year reports of adult support and their subsequent attendance at Epsilon Middle. A secondary analysis will examine identity acceptance. The study will also describe how these reported experiences vary across student populations, subject to adequate measurement quality, sample size, and confidentiality protections.

An adjusted association describes a comparison under the specified model and its assumptions. Covariate adjustment does not automatically remove confounding or turn this observational study into an estimate of an intervention’s effect (Lundberg et al., 2021).

**The purpose is not to prove that the principal caused—or can eliminate—the attendance gap.** The intended contribution is a more precise understanding of associations involving conditions that school leaders can investigate.

### 2. Construct the theoretical framework around leadership and school experience

#### Model theoretical-framework narrative

This study is guided by **Culturally Responsive School Leadership**, supplemented by a critical conceptualization of school belonging and an equity-oriented approach to quantitative research. Together, these perspectives direct attention toward how students experience the institution, how those experiences are measured, and how statistical differences are interpreted.

Culturally Responsive School Leadership supplies the central leadership perspective. Khalifa et al. (2016) identify critical self-awareness, culturally responsive school environments, and community advocacy as important dimensions of leadership practice. This perspective moves the inquiry beyond whether students comply with attendance expectations and toward whether school relationships and organizational conditions are responsive to their experiences.

Mansfield and Lambrinou (2024) further emphasize that culturally responsive schooling requires organizational support rather than individual commitment alone. Their analysis directs attention toward communication, relationships, student voice, and institutional planning. Applied here, leadership is understood through the conditions students encounter—not simply through administrators’ descriptions of their intentions.

Within that orientation, **adult support** concerns students’ perceived connections with school adults: whether adults are available, responsive, and experienced as supportive. This is a student-level experience that may vary within a school. It is not equivalent to an overall rating of principal effectiveness.

**Identity acceptance** concerns whether students experience recognition and acceptance of who they are. Kuttner (2023) argues that belonging is more than an individual psychological feeling; it also involves social relationships, institutional arrangements, power, and inclusion. Consequently, this study will not interpret lower reported acceptance as a student’s failure to “fit in.” It will treat such reports as information about the student–institution relationship.

Recent empirical research provides a reason to investigate these relationships, although it does not settle the Epsilon question. Graham et al. (2026) found associations between aggregated student perceptions of school climate and school-level absenteeism in Georgia, with connectedness and safety particularly relevant. Because their analysis concerns aggregated school measures, its findings motivate—but do not establish—the individual-level associations proposed in this thesis.

Community Cultural Wealth supplies an additional interpretive safeguard. Acevedo and Solorzano (2023) emphasize the knowledge and resources Communities of Color draw upon while navigating educational settings and racism. Accordingly, this study will not treat an attendance disparity or a low survey score as evidence that students and families lack commitment, knowledge, or capacity. Nor will it assume that racial identity, English Learner classification, disability, and foster-care status are interchangeable categories.

Finally, an equity-oriented quantitative approach makes measurement and categorization part of the inquiry. Castillo and Babb’s (2024) review of QuantCrit research highlights attention to community, racial categorization, researcher positioning, and interpretations that do not automatically center White students as the standard. These principles inform the study’s treatment of racialized experiences; they are not presented as a complete theory of disability or foster-care experiences.

#### What this framework changes

The framework changes the study from:

> “Which kinds of students are most likely to be absent?”

to:

> **“How are leadership-relevant school experiences distributed, and how are those experiences associated with later attendance?”**

Student-group information remains important for examining inequity. However, a reporting category is not treated as an explanation of a student’s behavior.

The framework also prevents the researcher from claiming to have measured all of culturally responsive leadership with a brief student survey. The study examines **selected student experiences relevant to leadership**, not the entirety of the leadership model.

### 3. Translate the theoretical framework into a conceptual framework

The theoretical framework identifies what deserves attention. The conceptual framework specifies what this study will actually examine.

#### Model conceptual-framework narrative

The conceptual framework identifies two measured aspects of students’ school experiences: perceived adult support and perceived identity acceptance. These experiences will be assessed early in the academic year and examined in relation to attendance during a subsequent observation period.

The framework proposes that students who experience stronger adult connections or greater identity acceptance may have different subsequent attendance patterns. These are theoretical expectations to investigate, not established causal relationships.

Prior attendance and other prespecified baseline characteristics will provide context for the analysis. A student’s earlier attendance may be associated with both current school relationships and later absence. The framework therefore distinguishes the focal school-experience measures from relevant conditions that precede their measurement.

The primary analysis will estimate the association between adult support and subsequent attendance. Identity acceptance will be examined in a separate secondary analysis. A joint model may be used exploratorily, but it will not be interpreted as demonstrating that belonging mediates a leadership effect.

Because the study includes one school, it cannot estimate the effect of attending a school with one principal rather than another. Its evidence concerns variation among students’ experiences **within Epsilon Middle**.

**Table 2. From theory to concepts and study variables**

| **Theoretical contribution** | **Concept examined** | **Quantitative application** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Culturally Responsive School Leadership | Experienced connection with supportive adults | Early-year adult-connection survey score |
| Critical understanding of belonging | Experienced acceptance of identity | Early-year identity-acceptance survey score |
| Evidence-bound leadership inquiry | Subsequent attendance outcome | Absence records collected after the survey period |
| Equity-oriented quantitative inquiry | Distribution of experiences and outcomes | Carefully qualified subgroup descriptions |
| Community-accountable leadership | Meaning and relevance of measurement | Student and family input into instrument review and interpretation |

*Note.* This is the proposed study’s conceptual translation, not a table reproduced from a published theory. The distinction between concepts and the quantities estimated follows Lundberg et al.’s (2021) emphasis on connecting statistical targets explicitly to theory.

The last row is important. Community participation does not disappear because the thesis is quantitative. Students and families can help identify confusing questions, challenge interpretations, and determine whether findings address meaningful concerns. Such consultation does not automatically create a separate qualitative research strand.

### 4. Specify the research questions and hypotheses

A quantitative thesis should distinguish questions about **distribution**, **association**, and **causation**. This study addresses the first two.

#### Research Question 1: Distribution

**How do reported adult support and identity acceptance vary across the participating students and the student groups that can be examined responsibly?**

This question concerns the distribution of school experiences. It does not presume that every member of a reporting category has the same experience.

#### Research Question 2: Primary association

**What is the association between early-year adult support and subsequent frequent absence, after accounting for prespecified baseline characteristics?**

The theoretical expectation is that stronger adult support will be associated with lower odds of subsequent frequent absence.

The primary null hypothesis is:

$$
H_0: \beta_{\text{adult support}} = 0
$$

The alternative hypothesis is:

$$
H_A: \beta_{\text{adult support}} \ne 0
$$

Here, $H_0$ is the null hypothesis and $H_A$ is the alternative. The coefficient $\beta_{\text{adult support}}$ represents the adult-support association on the model’s log-odds scale; it corresponds to $\beta_1$ in the later regression equation. A zero coefficient means no conditional association on that scale under the specified model, not proof that adult support has no educational value.

The analysis would use a two-sided test, despite the directional expectation. An association in the unexpected direction would require interpretation rather than being discarded.

#### Research Question 3: Secondary association

**What is the association between early-year identity acceptance and subsequent frequent absence, using the same baseline adjustment strategy?**

The expectation is again a negative association. This secondary analysis would be identified in advance rather than introduced only after examining the results.

The thesis would not begin with hypotheses asserting that English Learner, disability, or foster-care status inherently produces absenteeism. Those categories describe aspects of students’ circumstances and institutional classifications; they do not supply the mechanism being investigated.

### 5. Decide what data the study actually requires

#### The aggregate app data are insufficient for this analysis

The synthetic app data provide separate aggregate summaries. They do not tell us which students reported stronger adult connections, which of those students were later absent, or how their characteristics overlap.

Therefore, the researcher cannot calculate the proposed individual-level association from the subgroup rates.

**The 720 students represented in the synthetic school total must not be converted into 720 invented student records and then analyzed as though they were observations.** Creating a coherent individual-level simulation would be a separate exercise, with its assumptions explicitly identified.

A real version of this thesis would require authorized, linked student-level records and newly collected survey evidence.

#### Proposed observation schedule

For this example, the study would cover grades 6–8 and use the following schedule.

**Table 3. Data collection and timing**

| **Stage** | **Evidence collected** | **Purpose** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before the survey | Prior-year attendance, grade, and approved baseline characteristics | Establish conditions preceding the focal measures |
| Early-year survey window | Adult support and identity-acceptance responses | Measure the school experiences of interest |
| After the survey window | Days absent and days expected to attend through year-end | Measure subsequent attendance |
| End-of-study review | Participation, missingness, transfers, and record-linkage information | Identify exclusions and limits to interpretation |

Separating the survey from the subsequent attendance period prevents a misleading sequence in which the study claims that a survey predicted absences that had already occurred. It does **not**, by itself, eliminate confounding or prove causation.

#### Define the attendance outcome precisely

For this proposed study, the primary outcome would be **frequent absence during the follow-up period**, defined as missing at least 10% of the instructional days the student was expected to attend after the survey window.

$$
Y_i =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } A_i / E_i \geq 0.10, \\
0, & \text{if } A_i / E_i < 0.10.
\end{cases}
$$

Here, $i$ identifies a student, $A_i$ is the number of days absent during follow-up, and $E_i$ is the number of instructional days that student was expected to attend during follow-up. The indicator $Y_i$ is defined only for an eligible student with an evaluable attendance record; missing or insufficient observation is not coded as zero.

California’s official chronic-absenteeism reporting also uses a 10% threshold, with specified eligibility rules. However, this study’s post-survey observation window is not the same as the official annual reporting period. The thesis must label it as a **study-defined follow-up outcome**, not claim to reproduce the app’s annual indicator (California Department of Education [CDE], n.d.-b).

The proposed protocol would require at least 31 expected instructional days during follow-up. Students with shorter observed periods would not be assigned a zero. Their limited observation would be recorded and reported.

Transfers and incomplete records would also require attention. A student’s departure from the observed school must not be interpreted as improved attendance.

#### Choose the outcome representation before choosing the model

The 10% threshold is one defensible instructional choice, not the only way to represent attendance. Dichotomization loses information: students missing 11% and 30% of expected days both receive a value of 1, while students just below and just above the threshold receive different values. A real study should choose the representation from its research question, target quantity, observation period, and data—not from familiarity with logistic regression (Lundberg et al., 2021).

**Table 4. Alternative attendance outcomes to consider at the design stage**

| Representation | Question it could address | What must be specified |
|---|---|---|
| **Binary frequent-absence outcome** | Who meets the prespecified threshold during follow-up? | Threshold, observation eligibility, and the intended comparison |
| **Proportion of expected days absent** | How much of the observed attendance opportunity was missed? | Denominator, observation length, and an analysis appropriate to the distribution |
| **Absence count with exposure** | How does the frequency of absence differ relative to expected attendance days? | Expected days as exposure and an appropriate count-model specification |
| **Time to a prespecified absence event** | When is a clearly defined absence event or threshold first reached? | Event rule, time origin, follow-up, and censoring arrangements |

These are alternative design possibilities, not additional analyses that must all be performed. This example retains its binary primary outcome. Any planned sensitivity analysis should be justified and distinguished from post hoc attempts to obtain a preferred result.

### 6. Measure the concepts rather than invent a “leadership score”

#### Adult support and identity acceptance

Stickl Haugen et al. (2025) developed the **Student Belonging Scale** with middle and high school students. Its Results and Discussion distinguish a 22-item, four-factor solution from a 40-item development pool. The four domains are Connection with Adults, Connection with Peers, Identity Acceptance by Others, and Academic Behaviors. This is initial measurement evidence, not automatic validation for a new setting.

For this thesis, the published **Connection with Adults** and **Identity Acceptance by Others** subscales would be candidates for measuring the two focal constructs, subject to permission, review of the full instrument, and verification of its scoring instructions.

> **Instrument-use boundary**
>
> Verify the authorized final instrument and scoring before use: the article’s response-scale and score-range descriptions are not fully aligned. This tutorial does not infer a response range or reproduce a scoring key; resolve the discrepancy with the instrument or its authors (Stickl Haugen et al., 2025).

The study would not automatically use the total scale score. Including academic-behavior items in a predictor of attendance could blur the distinction between the proposed explanatory construct and the outcome. Keeping the selected domains separate makes their interpretation clearer.

#### What local measurement review would involve

Before the main survey, the researcher would examine whether students understand the items as intended, whether translations and accessibility arrangements preserve their meaning, and whether the published factor structure is plausible in the local sample.

The analysis plan would evaluate dimensionality and score reliability. It would not treat a high reliability coefficient as proof that a scale measures every aspect of culturally responsive leadership.

Group comparisons require additional care. The researcher should examine whether the measure functions comparably across the groups being compared. Where the sample cannot support that examination—particularly for a small foster-youth subgroup—the study should qualify or limit claims about differences in the underlying construct.

If items are rewritten substantially, the resulting instrument must be identified as an adaptation requiring new evidence. It cannot simply inherit the original instrument’s validation.

#### A useful distinction for the student

An administrator’s statement that support is available measures **reported availability**.

A student’s account of being able to obtain help measures **experienced access or connection**.

A service log measures **documented activity**.

Those are related but different forms of evidence. The proposed thesis concentrates on student-reported experiences and does not assume that one source substitutes for the others.

### 7. Plan recruitment and sample size around the analysis

The synthetic dataset contains 720 students, but that does not establish the sample size of a future study. Participation, permissions, survey completion, record linkage, and follow-up eligibility would determine the usable sample.

The proposed study would invite all eligible students rather than recruit only students already classified as chronically absent. Restricting recruitment to students with prior chronic absenteeism would change the target population and limit conclusions about the full eligible student population, even though subsequent attendance could still vary within that restricted sample.

The protocol would provide multiple participation opportunities within the baseline survey window, including accessible make-up arrangements. Otherwise, a study about absenteeism could systematically miss students absent on the survey day.

#### Justify the sample before collecting outcomes

Lakens (2022) emphasizes aligning sample-size justification with the information the analysis is intended to provide. For this study, planning should consider the smallest association worth detecting, the likely number of frequent-absence cases, the number of baseline parameters, clustering, and anticipated missingness—not simply a generic rule that a certain number of students is “enough.”

A simulation-based power or precision analysis would be appropriate for the planned model. Its assumptions would need to be documented before examining the outcome associations.

The synthetic foster-youth count of 28 illustrates an important limitation. Even a schoolwide invitation may produce too few participants in a particular group to estimate an interaction or separate regression reliably. A group’s educational importance does not make its estimate statistically precise.

The response should not be to combine unrelated groups merely to obtain a smaller *p* value. It may be more defensible to retain carefully protected descriptive information, acknowledge uncertainty, and identify the need for a larger or differently designed study.

### 8. Connect the conceptual framework to the analysis

#### First, describe what was actually collected

The analysis would begin with participant counts, response rates, missing data, scale distributions, and attendance coverage.

The researcher would distinguish the baseline eligible population from survey respondents and from students included in the linked analysis. Where authorized, baseline characteristics could be compared to assess how participants differ from those not represented.

Subgroup summaries would remain descriptive. English Learners, Students with Disabilities, and Foster Youth are overlapping categories. They should not be entered into a one-way analysis as though they were mutually exclusive groups.

Equity-oriented interpretation also requires explaining whose experiences are represented and how categories were constructed, rather than presenting numerical labels as natural divisions (Castillo & Babb, 2024).

#### Then estimate the primary association

Because the proposed attendance outcome is binary, a logistic regression model would estimate the association between adult support and the odds of frequent absence.

A planned multilevel form is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{logit}(p_{ij}) &= \log\!\left(\frac{p_{ij}}{1-p_{ij}}\right) \\
&= \beta_0 + \beta_1 S_{ij} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{C}_{ij} + u_j.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here, $i$ identifies a student and $j$ identifies an advisory group. The term $p_{ij}$ is the modeled probability of frequent absence, and $\operatorname{logit}(p_{ij})$ expresses that probability as log odds. The intercept $\beta_0$ is the model’s reference log odds when predictors and the group term are zero. The adult-support score is $S_{ij}$, and $\beta_1$ is its conditional coefficient for the prespecified score unit. The vector $\mathbf{C}_{ij}$ contains baseline covariates; $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ contains their coefficients, and the superscript $\mathsf T$ indicates the transpose used to form their summed contribution. The advisory-group term $u_j$ allows baseline attendance patterns to vary across groups.

In plain language, the model asks whether students with different reported adult support have different later attendance odds under the chosen baseline adjustment and grouping structure. It does not calculate the effect of improving school leadership.

The actual number and structure of advisory groups must be established during design. A multilevel model should not be promised without checking whether the sample supports it. The clustering strategy would be finalized before outcome analysis, and there would be no school-level random effect because the study contains only one school.

A parsimonious baseline set could include prior attendance, grade, and documented pre-baseline enrollment mobility. Additional variables would require a substantive reason and sufficient information—not selection through an automated search for statistical significance.

#### What is the target quantity?

The primary target is the **adjusted association between adult support and subsequent frequent absence among students represented in the analytic cohort**.

The researcher would report the odds ratio for a specified score difference, such as one baseline standard deviation. Model-based attendance probabilities would also help readers understand practical magnitude.

This target must remain distinct from “the reduction in absenteeism that would occur if the principal improved support.” The latter is an intervention-effect question that this observational design does not identify. Lundberg et al. (2021) make this distinction between the quantity a study seeks to estimate and the assumptions required to connect evidence to that quantity central to quantitative research design.

#### Analyze identity acceptance separately

A secondary model would replace adult support with identity acceptance while retaining the same planned baseline adjustment.

An exploratory joint model could include both. However, if the coefficients change, the researcher would not automatically conclude that one variable explains or mediates the other. A joint association model is not a causal mediation analysis.

The primary test, secondary analyses, and exploratory comparisons would be identified before results were examined. The thesis should not present whichever analysis produces the smallest *p* value as though it had always been the central question.

### 9. Address missing data, uncertainty, and alternative explanations

The proposed analysis would not replace missing survey answers, unlinked attendance records, or suppressed information with zero.

The protocol would distinguish item nonresponse, entire-survey nonresponse, transfers, and unavailable baseline records. These are different problems and may have different implications.

Where justified, missing-data methods could be used under explicitly stated assumptions, accompanied by sensitivity analyses. Such methods would not be described as eliminating bias from students who never participated or from unobserved reasons for missingness.

Several alternative explanations would remain relevant even after baseline adjustment. Earlier attendance difficulties may shape current adult relationships. Students receiving intensive support may have greater needs than students receiving little support. Unmeasured health, transportation, or housing conditions may relate to both perceived support and attendance.

These possibilities do not make the analysis useless. They determine the strength of the conclusion.

A defensible result would be:

> “Adult support was associated with subsequent attendance under the specified model and study conditions.”

It would not be:

> “The analysis proved that poor leadership caused the attendance gap.”

The study’s emphasis on institutional conditions must therefore be matched by restraint in attributing institutional causation.

#### What the quantitative findings section must report

A reader needs enough information to understand who is represented, what was measured, what was estimated, and how uncertainty was handled. Use the following reporting guide with the actual study results; it is not an instruction to generate additional analyses without a design rationale.

**Table 5. Quantitative reporting guide**

| Reporting element | Information to include |
|---|---|
| **Sample and observation** | Eligible population, respondents, linked analytic sample, exclusions, transfers, and follow-up coverage |
| **Missing evidence** | Item nonresponse, whole-survey nonresponse, missing records, assumptions, and the handling of each |
| **Measurement** | Instrument version, score construction, score unit, measurement evidence, and limits to group comparisons |
| **Model specification** | Outcome, exposure or predictor, baseline covariates, grouping structure, and assumptions |
| **Magnitude and uncertainty** | Effect estimate, confidence interval, and an interpretable probability or other magnitude with its stated comparison |
| **Robustness and analytic status** | Diagnostics and justified sensitivity checks; clearly identified planned, secondary, and exploratory analyses |
| **Interpretation** | Alternative explanations, representativeness, and the boundary between association and intervention effect |

The target quantity should remain visible throughout this reporting, rather than being inferred from whichever coefficient is most statistically noticeable (Lakens, 2022; Lundberg et al., 2021).

### 10. Illustrate how results would be interpreted

**The following numbers are invented to demonstrate interpretation. They were not calculated from the Epsilon data or collected from students.**

**Table 6. Illustrative—not observed—regression results**

| **Analysis** | **Odds ratio per one-standard-deviation higher score** | **95% confidence interval** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary: Adult support | 0.68 | 0.50–0.93 |
| Secondary: Identity acceptance | 0.84 | 0.62–1.13 |

*Note.* The rows represent separate hypothetical models with the same prespecified baseline covariates. No actual study results are being reported.

#### Interpreting the adult-support association

An odds ratio of 0.68 would indicate **32% lower conditional odds** of frequent absence for a one-standard-deviation higher adult-support score under the model.

It would **not** mean a 32-percentage-point reduction in absenteeism or a 32% reduction in probability.

For illustration, if a particular modeled student profile had a 25% probability, multiplying its odds by 0.68 would correspond to a probability of approximately 18.5%. That calculation illustrates the difference between odds and probability; it is not an Epsilon prediction.

#### Interpreting the identity-acceptance association

The hypothetical identity-acceptance interval includes 1.00. That would indicate uncertainty about the direction and magnitude of the association under this analysis.

It would not justify declaring that identity acceptance “does not matter.” The estimate could be imprecise, the measure could be limited, or the selected outcome and time period might not capture its importance. Sample-size and precision planning help determine what conclusions a study can reasonably support (Lakens, 2022).

#### Model findings narrative

> In the illustrative primary analysis, stronger adult support was associated with lower odds of frequent absence after adjustment for the prespecified baseline characteristics. The corresponding estimate for identity acceptance was closer to an odds ratio of 1.00, and its confidence interval included both lower and higher odds of frequent absence. These patterns would support closer examination of students’ experiences of adult relationships, but they would not establish that changing those relationships would produce a specified improvement in attendance. The observational design, measurement limitations, and potential for unmeasured differences would remain part of the interpretation.

A real thesis would replace this paragraph with actual estimates, diagnostics, sample information, and appropriately bounded findings.

### 11. Preserve community accountability in a quantitative thesis

A survey can make student experience more visible, but it also limits students to the questions and response options the researcher has provided.

For this proposed study, a small student and family advisory process would review the relevance and clarity of the measures, identify important omissions, and discuss how aggregate findings should be interpreted. Participants would not be asked to approve the researcher’s preferred explanation.

This involvement would support the instrument and interpretation rather than constitute a separate qualitative findings strand. If the researcher systematically collected and analyzed interviews to answer additional research questions, the design would need to be reconsidered as a mixed-methods study.

The protocol would also separate participation from grades, discipline, access to services, and staff evaluation. Identifiable research records would remain in an approved research environment, not the public application.

Results would be reported with protections against identifying students through combinations of small-group membership and distinctive characteristics. Small cells would not be reconstructed to make a complete table. CDE’s public attendance files themselves apply suppression rules to protect student privacy; a research project must also establish its own approved protections for the data it collects (CDE, n.d.-a).

### 12. Translate the findings back into leadership decisions

The quantitative model is one part of the leadership inquiry, not its conclusion.

**Table 7. Applying the six-question leadership model**

| **Leadership move** | **Quantitative application** | **Claim boundary** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Describe the pattern** | Report attendance outcomes and school-experience distributions. | Description does not establish cause. |
| **Locate variation** | Examine adequately measured, responsibly reported differences. | Categories overlap and may conceal internal variation. |
| **State the boundary** | Document measurement, missingness, precision, and design limitations. | Adjustment does not eliminate every alternative explanation. |
| **Include community knowledge** | Review measures and interpretations with students and families. | Survey categories do not contain every relevant experience. |
| **Investigate the system** | Identify practices that warrant closer implementation review. | A perception score is not a complete measure of policy or leadership. |
| **Decide and learn** | Recommend a bounded next inquiry or monitored improvement effort. | An association does not establish an intervention’s effectiveness. |

*Note.* The leadership sequence is adapted from the white paper; the quantitative applications are proposed for this example.

Suppose actual evidence eventually resembled the illustrative adult-support result. A defensible leadership response might be to examine which students can reliably reach a supportive adult, how advisory responsibilities operate, and whether existing support processes are experienced differently.

The response would not be to announce that a mentoring program has been proven to solve the attendance disparity.

A subsequent improvement effort could test a carefully specified change, monitor implementation and student experience, and examine attendance over time. That would be a further study or improvement cycle—not an effect already demonstrated by the thesis.

### 13. How the thesis chapters would connect

**Chapter 1** would establish the attendance concern, identify what the aggregate evidence cannot answer, and present the purpose, questions, and hypotheses.

**Chapter 2** would explain the leadership-centered theoretical framework, the belonging concepts, and the study-specific conceptual relationships. It would distinguish theory from the variables used to represent selected aspects of it.

**Chapter 3** would specify the longitudinal observational design, population, recruitment, measures, observation windows, sample-size justification, model, missing-data plan, and participant protections.

**Chapter 4** would present actual measurement findings, descriptive results, model estimates, confidence intervals, and sensitivity analyses. It would distinguish planned analyses from exploratory ones.

**Chapter 5** would relate the findings to the framework, examine alternative explanations, and identify proportionate leadership implications.

The central teaching distinction is:

> **The theoretical framework identifies which institutional experiences deserve investigation. The conceptual framework turns those experiences into specific, measurable relationships. Quantitative analysis estimates those relationships. The leadership model determines how cautiously—and how usefully—the findings should inform the next decision.**
